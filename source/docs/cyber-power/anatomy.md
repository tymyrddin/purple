# The anatomy of cyber power

Ask where a state's cyber power sits and the obvious answer is an organisation: a military command, an intelligence
service, a government bureau, perhaps a contractor with a government contract behind it. The cases in this series keep
making that answer inadequate.

North Korea turns cyber theft into revenue because its access to ordinary finance is constrained. Russia leaves a
criminal economy partly intact and reaches into it when useful. China turns a technical market into an extension of
state demand. Israel turns military selection into a labour market. The United States makes persistent access into
standing government practice. Lithuania distributes capability across institutions and alliances. France keeps the stack
national. The European Union builds machinery around capabilities it cannot own. Estonia makes the state itself a
digital system. Ukraine shows what happens when cyber becomes inseparable from war.

The differences are substantial, but the cases can still be read through the same relationships. Something creates the
demand for capability. Something turns that demand into action. Capability accumulates somewhere, then moves through
people, contracts, institutions or alliances. Something limits what it can do. And the capability feeds back into the
conditions that produced it.

That is why counting organisations tells only part of the story. Cyber power is not simply something an actor has. It is
something produced and reproduced by the relationships around it.

## Conditions first

The conditions come before the capability, and they are usually not technological.

[North Korea](signatures/treasury-as-target.md) needs foreign currency because sanctions constrain ordinary finance.
[Russia](signatures/boundary-that-does-not-hold.md) has a large skilled population and a criminal economy that the
state has chosen to live alongside rather than suppress. [China](signatures/banality-on-the-invoice.md) has industrial
policy and a commercial technical market capable of supplying what state programmes require.
[Iran](signatures/operating-under-ceiling.md) faces adversaries whose retaliation it cannot afford to absorb.
[Israel](signatures/the-revolving-door.md) has conscription that sorts a large population, trains it intensively and
releases it into civilian life while retaining the ability to call it back.
The [United States](signatures/permanent-contact.md) combines scale with global reach and a dense network of alliances.
[Lithuania](signatures/no-single-hand.md) has exposure without strategic depth.

The European cases produce different conditions again. [France](european-mesh/france.md) inherits a strategic culture
built around the ability to act without another state's permission. The [European Union](european-mesh/eu.md) operates
under a constitutional settlement that leaves national security with the member states.
[Estonia](next-boundary/estonia.md) built a state whose essential functions became digital.
[Ukraine](next-boundary/ukraine.md) is fighting a war through a society whose government, communications and economy
already depend heavily on digital infrastructure.

None of these conditions is cyber in itself. They are fiscal, strategic, demographic, legal or geographic. Cyber
capability takes the shape that those conditions make useful.

## Mundane mechanisms

Between a condition and an operation there is usually no secret ingredient. There is an administrative mechanism.

North Korea needs payrolls, front companies, exchanges and laundering routes to turn stolen assets into money the state
can use. Russia needs a degree of tolerance, because a criminal crew that remains outside prosecution is a crew the
state can later reach. China uses procurement, contracts and commercial incentives to turn state requirements into work
for contractors. The American mechanism is doctrine and standing authority, which turns persistent contact into routine
government business. Israel selects people through military service, releases them into the economy and retains a
reserve system capable of pulling them back.

Lithuania makes usefulness into a mechanism: a state too small to hoard capability can make itself valuable to larger
networks and gain reach through the same relationships. France uses law and doctrine to assign authority and keep the
stack national. The Union uses regulation, coordination machinery and sanctions because those are the instruments
available to an institution that does not control the national capabilities beneath it.

These mechanisms are strikingly ordinary. Payroll, contract, licence, doctrine, regulation, reserve obligation. They are
also where a posture becomes reproducible. A capability can change when the machinery that produces it changes, even
when the technology remains the same.

## No single container

Capability has to accumulate somewhere, but there is no general answer to where.

North Korea comes closest to a conventional container, with a state-directed apparatus that can be traced through
bureaux, personnel and government reporting. Israel's capability accumulates in people and in the companies those people
later build. China's accumulates in a market of contractors whose work can be commissioned, sold and reused. American
capability runs across military and intelligence organisations, government agencies, private companies and allied
systems.

Lithuania is different again. The network is the concentration. France concentrates capability deliberately, insisting
that doctrine, command, intelligence, defence and industrial capacity remain nationally held. The Union does almost the
reverse, building coordination machinery around capabilities that remain with the member states.

Estonia puts essential state capability into registries, digital services and the infrastructure that allows them to
remain available. Ukraine's digital infrastructure is now part of the state's ability to continue functioning during
war.

The question "how much cyber capability does this state have?" quietly assumes that capability has a container. In
several of these cases, the more useful question is where the capability is distributed and what holds the pieces
together.

## Capability in transit

Capability also has a route.

Israeli expertise can move from military unit to veteran to start-up to product and back into military service through
reserve mobilisation. Chinese capability can move from a state requirement to a contractor and then into another market.
Russian capability can move between criminal enterprise and state tasking without requiring a clean institutional
boundary between the two.

Lithuania sends capability outward into European rapid-response teams and regional defence arrangements, while allied
teams can move inward when Lithuania needs assistance. Ukraine has moved government data and services into foreign
infrastructure. Estonia has maintained copies of critical state data outside the country. France's sovereign cloud
arrangements rely on foreign technology beneath French operation. The Union's sanctions, regulations and coordination
mechanisms move decisions across twenty-seven national systems without moving the underlying national capabilities into
Brussels.

Movement changes the meaning of ownership. A person can carry capability from a state into a company and back again. A
contract can carry it from a government requirement into a commercial service. An alliance can make one state's
capability available to another without transferring ownership. A cloud platform can make a foreign technology part of a
national system without making the system foreign in the legal sense.

The busiest cyber systems are often the ones in which "whose capability?" has no simple answer.

## Limits are part of the form

Every form has a limit, but the limits differ because the conditions differ.

Iran's limit is the risk of provoking a response it cannot absorb. Its repertoire therefore favours effects that can
often be denied, reversed or disowned, although Albania and the later conflicts show how uncertain that calibration can
become.

North Korea's activity produces unusually extensive external scrutiny through indictments, sanctions and international
reporting. The same monitoring that makes the apparatus visible also changes the conditions under which it operates.

The Union's limit is sovereign consent. A collective decision can depend on twenty-seven governments agreeing to it.
France's limit is the floor beneath its sovereign stack: hyperscale cloud, advanced semiconductors and allied
intelligence remain outside complete national control.

The American limit is harder to see. Its doctrine is visible, but much of the activity that would most directly test its
offensive posture is not. What outsiders can observe is therefore systematically different from the whole apparatus.

The limit is not simply a fence around the capability. It comes from the same condition that produced the capability.
Sanctions make North Korean theft necessary and make it legible. Strategic autonomy builds France's stack and exposes
its dependence on foreign foundations. Standing authority makes American contact routine while leaving the most
consequential uses of that authority difficult to observe.

The constraint is part of the architecture.

## The return path

The relationship does not run in one direction. Capability changes the condition that produced it, and the changed
condition produces more capability.

North Korean theft helps finance the state while reinforcing the sanctions environment that makes external revenue
necessary. Russian tolerance preserves a pool of people and organisations the state can later use. Israeli veterans
create companies, and those companies alter the civilian market into which the next cohort is released. Lithuanian
usefulness deepens its alliance relationships, and those relationships increase the reach that makes Lithuania useful.

France's investment in autonomy creates institutions and industries that make further autonomy easier to justify. The
Union's coordination produces interoperability and trust, which can make further cooperation possible. Estonia's
digitalisation makes the state easier to operate across distance and, at the same time, makes digital resilience part of
national resilience. American persistence changes what adversaries learn, and their adaptation changes the environment
in which the American apparatus operates.

The result is not necessarily a deliberate strategy. A system can reproduce itself without anyone deciding that it ought
to. Once the capability has altered the condition around it, the original arrangement can become self-reinforcing.

## Where categories stop

The boundaries around these systems are as revealing as the systems themselves.

Russia sits across the boundary between state and crime. China between state and contractor. Israel between military and
market. The United States between peace and war. Lithuania between national capability and alliance network. France
between sovereignty and dependence. The European Union between national authority and collective action. Estonia between
state and infrastructure. Ukraine between cyber and war.

Some [boundaries are deliberately maintained](boundaries-move.md). France keeps defensive and offensive cyber missions 
institutionally separate. The Union keeps national security with the member states. Others become porous because the 
system works through them.

This is why [the actor is often the wrong unit of analysis](actor-is-not-the-unit.md). A state can be the legal owner 
of a capability without being its sole source, operator or destination. A contractor can perform state work without 
becoming part of the state. A network can exercise capability without anyone in the network owning the whole. A digital 
infrastructure can become part of the state without looking like a state institution.

The cases therefore do not resolve into a larger list of cyber actors. They point in the other direction. Cyber power
appears wherever conditions, mechanisms, accumulation, movement, limits and feedback hold together long enough to
produce an effect.

Sometimes that arrangement has a name. Sometimes it has a flag. Sometimes it has a contract, a market, an alliance or a
piece of infrastructure.

The name is useful. It is not the system.
