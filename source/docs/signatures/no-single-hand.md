# No single hand

*Lithuania, and cyber power as a network rather than an actor*

A small state on NATO's eastern edge appears in the cyber story in one of two roles: as a victim, on the receiving end
of Russian denial-of-service raids, or as a dutiful member ticking the alliance's boxes. Lithuania has been both. In
June
2022 [pro-Russian hacktivists](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/252522092/Russia-aligned-hacktivists-behind-Lithuania-DDoS-attack)
knocked its state networks offline in retaliation for its enforcement of the sanctions blockade to Kaliningrad. It runs
a [national cyber-security centre](https://www.nksc.lt/en/), a national response team, the usual furniture.

But the striking thing about Lithuanian cyber power is that there is no Lithuanian equivalent of Lazarus or Sandworm to
name, and the absence is itself the signature. A small, exposed state with no strategic depth does not build an
autonomous offensive apparatus. It does the opposite: it disperses its cyber capability across institutions and
alliances, and pushes it outward. The unit of analysis is not an actor. It is a mesh.

## Exposure without depth

The substrate is exposure without depth. Lithuania is small, borders Russian Kaliningrad and Belarus, and remembers
occupation; it also picked a quarrel with China over Taiwan and paid for it in trade. A state in that position cannot
hold a large independent capability and cannot afford to be caught short. What it can do is make itself useful to a much
larger security system, and become, on the frontline, the part of that system that notices first.

## The coupling: distribute, and push outward

So the coupling runs the other way from the offensive cases. Where a larger state concentrates capability, Lithuania
distributes and exports it.
It [leads the EU's cyber rapid-response teams](https://www.lrt.lt/en/news-in-english/19/2200650/lithuania-takes-over-chairmanship-of-eu-s-cyber-security-force),
a multinational unit it has sent to help Ukraine and Moldova.
It [hosts a regional cyber-defence centre](https://www.eucybernet.eu/project/rcdc/) built with the United States,
designed so that non-member partners such as Ukraine and Georgia can plug in. When its national centre took apart a
batch of Chinese handsets in 2021 and found a Xiaomi device quietly able to censor phrases like "Free Tibet," the
government [told people to get rid of their Chinese phones](https://therecord.media/lithuanian-government-warns-about-secret-censorship-features-in-xiaomi-phones),
and larger states cited the finding. A small state acts as the alliance's canary because it is small enough to move fast
and exposed enough to care. In 2025 it stood up
a [cyber command](https://kam.lt/en/lithuanian-cyber-defence-command-opened/) of its own, its mandate framed around
fitting into NATO rather than operating alone.

## Nobody to name

Look at who actually carries out a Lithuanian cyber action and the single actor dissolves. A national exercise team is
assembled from the defence ministry, the cyber-security centre, the armed forces, the criminal police, energy and
telecoms operators, the volunteer Riflemen's Union and the universities, and fielded jointly with the Netherlands. The
rapid-response team is European. The regional centre is bilateral with the United States and open to partners. There is
no core to point at, because the capability is constituted as a network spanning national, military, EU, bilateral,
commercial and volunteer bodies. The thing doing the work is a coalition, and calling it "Lithuania" is a convenience.

## Where the tidy version breaks

Two honest limits. The first is that all of this is defensive and resilience-shaped; there is no sourceable Lithuanian
offensive operation, and it would be dishonest to invent one to match the other chapters. The signature here is a
posture, not an offensive capability, and the asymmetry is real. The second is that a small state leading an alliance
project can be written up as a brochure, and the distinctiveness collapses the moment the mesh and the
distribute-not-hoard inversion are dropped. What keeps the case substantive is precisely that the acting entity is not a
state actor in the sense the other cases are.

## What stays open

Whether the dispersal is a chosen strategy or simply the only shape available to a state too small to hoard is not
something the record settles, and the two are hard to separate from outside. A larger exposed state might concentrate
what Lithuania distributes. Exposure plus alliance-embedding appears to produce a distributed, outward-facing
capability, but exposure and smallness arrive together here and cannot be pulled cleanly apart.

What survives is a single dispersal. The signature of this substrate is a network, not a group: cyber power constituted
across institutions and alliances and pushed toward the frontline rather than hoarded behind it. And the same question
follows for the others. If the thing doing the work here only looks like an actor once it is named, how much of Lazarus,
or Sandworm, or the contractor market, was ever a single hand either, and how much was the convenience of naming, seen
from the offensive side.
